## Problem and outcome

ENGR 100 required hardware that moved and sensed. I led two prototypes to
working demos: a **3D-printed solar vehicle chassis** that kept panel and
drivetrain packaging coherent, and a **moisture-sensing irrigation** loop that
waters when the soil asks — not on a blind timer.

## Role and context

- **Course:** ENGR 100, University of Michigan  
- **Role:** Hardware and prototyping lead on a student team  
- **What I personally owned:** chassis CAD / print iteration; irrigation sensor
  → MCU → actuator path and threshold behavior  
- **Team context:** classmates shared mechanical, electrical, and presentation
  work; I am describing the hardware thread I led  

## System / signal flow (irrigation)

```
moisture probe  →  MCU threshold (+ hysteresis)  →  valve / pump
     soil reading         decide wet vs dry              fluid only if dry
```

Solar vehicle work was primarily mechanical packaging: mounts, wall thickness,
print orientation, and fastener access as the drivetrain layout changed.

## Technical decisions and tradeoffs

**1. Treat the chassis as packaging, not sculpture.** Panel mass and motor
placement moved often. I prioritized mount points and print reliability over
cosmetic aero. Tradeoff: less "pretty" CAD; fewer cracked brackets at demo.

**2. Hysteresis on the moisture threshold.** Without it, the actuator chatters
at the wet/dry boundary. Tradeoff: slightly slower response; much less
mechanical wear and noise.

**3. Calibrate "wet" in real soil, not only in slides.** Probe readings drift;
oxide and placement matter. Tradeoff: more bench time; fewer false waterings
in the demo.

## Validation

- Both prototypes ran for course demo / evaluation  
- Irrigation: exercised threshold behavior with the probe (qualitative — I am
  not publishing numeric soil-% cutoffs without a logged dataset)  
- Chassis: survived assembly and demo handling after multiple print revisions
